## Q51

**Answer:** **insensibly**

**Reasoning:** The sentence describes earlier scientific assumptions about how reptiles imitate: they were thought to copy actions *without understanding their deeper significance*.  The adverb **insensibly** means “in a way that you cannot easily notice or feel”【100188319528614†L59-L66】, and by extension “without conscious perception.”  Saying reptiles *insensibly emulate actions* captures the idea that the imitation was assumed to be mindless or reflexive.  New observations of bearded dragons copying non‑instinctive actions prompt researchers to question that view.  The other adverbs either imply careful attention or emotional tone that does not match the description of mindless imitation.

| Option | Meaning and evaluation |
|---|---|
| **attentively** | *Attentively* means doing something with careful attention【774892750824597†L59-L61】.  Saying reptiles “attentively emulate actions” contradicts the earlier belief that they lacked awareness. |
| **insensibly** | Describes something happening in a way that is not noticed【100188319528614†L59-L66】; here it conveys the assumed unconsciousness of reptile imitation.  **Correct.** |
| **listlessly** | *Listlessly* means without energy or enthusiasm【489304327725419†L59-L60】.  This refers to lack of vigor, not lack of awareness, so it does not capture the idea of instinctive imitation. |
| **actively** | Doing something in an active, engaged way【309716967542839†L61-L64】.  This is the opposite of the context, which suggests unconscious mimicry. |
| **consciously** | *Consciously* means with awareness or intention【146071634542706†L59-L74】.  The sentence says reptiles were **not** thought to understand the actions’ significance, so consciously is wrong. |

## Q52

**Answer:** **inured to**

**Reasoning:** After years of war, people who witness constant bloodshed can become *accustomed* to suffering—so desensitized that they overlook misery around them.  The phrasal verb **inured to** means becoming familiar with something unpleasant so that it no longer affects you【823316736943842†L61-L65】.  The other options either suggest exemption, dominance or exaggerated emotion and do not convey this sense of hardened indifference.

| Option | Meaning and evaluation |
|---|---|
| **inured to** | To become used to something unpleasant【823316736943842†L61-L65】, fitting the idea of becoming desensitized. **Correct.** |
| **exempted from** | Being excused from a duty or requirement【611576562477212†L59-L61】; war survivors were not excused from suffering. |
| **dominant over** | Being the strongest or most important【19885043108523†L55-L67】; this describes power relations, not desensitization. |
| **effusive towards** | *Effusive* means showing unrestrained emotion or enthusiasm【757408840065125†L57-L59】; effusive toward suffering would mean overflowing with emotion, the opposite of being hardened. |
| **maudlin over** | *Maudlin* means tearfully sentimental【598481473215247†L30-L33】; again this is the opposite of the detached attitude described. |

## Q53

**Answer:** **(i) controversial habit; (ii) endorsed**

**Reasoning:** Exceeding the speed limit is ubiquitous in a state with long distances; human nature makes people drive fast even though it is illegal, leading to debate and frustration.  It is therefore a **controversial habit** rather than a “cherished tradition” (which suggests fond approval) or a “disquieting ritual” (which implies a grim ceremony).  Because the public demanded tougher enforcement, legislators unsurprisingly **endorsed** increased penalties.  *Acceding to public demand* implies support rather than merely considering or rejecting harsher penalties.

| Pair | Evaluation |
|---|---|
| **controversial habit / endorsed** | A *controversial habit* is a practice that provokes disagreement【878823683853252†L58-L63】, matching the idea that people speed even though it is debated.  To *endorse* means to approve or support; legislators endorsed stricter penalties in response to public demand.  **Correct.** |
| **cherished tradition / considered** | A *cherished tradition* is something loved and protected【141882222966234†L59-L62】; calling illegal speeding cherished makes legislators’ crackdown less plausible.  Moreover, merely considering penalties lacks the decisive tone implied by acceding to demand. |
| **disquieting ritual / rejected** | *Disquieting* means causing anxiety or unease【197447664581476†L27-L31】; while speeding may be worrying, pairing it with *rejected* implies legislators refused to impose penalties, contradicting the “acceding to public demand” clause. |

## Q54

**Answer:** **(i) roundly; (ii) panned**

**Reasoning:** Critics unanimously condemned the film; not a single reviewer had anything positive to say.  The adverb **roundly** means “severely or by a large number of people”【501541649407898†L61-L66】, often used with *criticized*.  The verb **panned** is colloquial for “criticized severely”【252697544738690†L26-L28】.  Thus, the film was **roundly panned**.  The other pairs either suggest praise (lauded, venerated) or dishonest criticism (mendaciously) and do not fit.

| Pair | Evaluation |
|---|---|
| **warily / lauded** | *Warily* means cautiously【13750844203751†L59-L62】 and *lauded* means praised【381374479808436†L65-L67】; this would mean critics cautiously praised the film, opposite of the context. |
| **mendaciously / panned** | *Mendaciously* means in a lying or dishonest manner【549522828820671†L28-L34】; criticising mendaciously implies dishonesty, which is not suggested by the passage. |
| **roundly / panned** | *Roundly* denotes thorough or severe criticism【501541649407898†L61-L66】 and *panned* means criticized harshly【252697544738690†L26-L28】.  This matches the statement that the film had no positive reviews. **Correct.** |
| **roundly / venerated** | Venerated means revered or highly respected, contradicting the description of unanimous disapproval. |

## Q55

**Answer:** **(i) exploiting; (ii) imperceptible; (iii) detected**

**Reasoning:** The researchers are taking advantage of the fact that laser printers leave unique banding patterns, so they are **exploiting** this fact rather than merely facing or manipulating it.  These patterns are **imperceptible**—too slight to be noticed—by the naked eye【774438371506314†L59-L60】.  However, computer programs can **detect** the patterns and analyze them.  “Implemented” and “generated” do not make sense because the patterns already exist.

| Slot | Option | Meaning and evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| **Blank (i)** | **exploiting** | To make use of a fact or resource for benefit; here researchers exploit the printer’s imperfections to trace documents. **Correct.** |
|  | facing | Means confronting or dealing with; the researchers are not merely confronting the fact but using it. |
|  | manipulating | Involves skilfully controlling or altering; they are not altering the printers but using existing flaws. |
| **Blank (ii)** | **imperceptible** | Not noticeable or too slight to be perceived【774438371506314†L59-L60】; the banding patterns are invisible to the naked eye. **Correct.** |
|  | obvious | Clearly visible; contradicts the statement that patterns are invisible. |
|  | legible | Readable; this describes handwriting or print clarity, not invisible patterns. |
| **Blank (iii)** | **detected** | Found or discovered; computer programs detect the patterns. **Correct.** |
|  | implemented | Means put into effect; not applicable because the patterns already exist. |
|  | generated | Means produced; again, the patterns are inherent in the printer’s output. |

## Q56

**Answer:** **inveigle**

**Reasoning:** The salesman used flattering talk in an attempt to persuade the indifferent couple to buy the luxury car.  **Inveigle** means “to win over by wiles, flattery or deception”【167263824613209†L96-L105】, fitting this context perfectly.  The other verbs either lack the sense of persuasion or carry inappropriate connotations.

| Option | Meaning and evaluation |
|---|---|
| **support** | To back or hold up; the salesman did not simply support the couple’s decision—he tried to persuade them. |
| **inveigle** | To entice or persuade by flattery or tricks【167263824613209†L96-L105】.  This matches the salesman’s attempt. **Correct.** |
| **deliberate** | To think or discuss carefully; this describes reasoning, not persuading someone else. |
| **marginalize** | To treat as insignificant; the salesman was flattering, not sidelining the couple. |
| **bully** | To intimidate or coerce; the salesman’s approach is described as flattering rather than bullying. |

## Q57

**Answer:** **susceptible to**

**Reasoning:** Early researchers thought the public was easily influenced by propaganda, implying a belief that people were **susceptible to** it.  **Susceptible** means easily influenced or harmed by something【958417814017260†L61-L64】.  Later studies show that mass communications seldom produce large changes, contradicting the earlier assumption.  None of the other options convey a sense of vulnerability to influence.

| Option | Meaning and evaluation |
|---|---|
| **unaware of** | Not knowing; early researchers did not argue ignorance but gullibility. |
| **scornful of** | Showing contempt; this would mean the public despised propaganda, opposite of being influenced. |
| **susceptible to** | Easily influenced or harmed【958417814017260†L61-L64】; fits the earlier claim that the public was prone to propaganda. **Correct.** |
| **unimpressed by** | Not affected; this is essentially the conclusion of the recent study, not the early assumption. |
| **coping with** | Dealing successfully with; again, not relevant to the claim of gullibility. |

## Q58

**Answer:** **(i) erroneously; (ii) unbeknownst**

**Reasoning:** A peanut is not technically a nut but a legume.  Therefore, it is **erroneously** (incorrectly) referred to as a nut; **unbeknownst** means “without someone knowing”【870173287945705†L73-L75】, capturing the idea that many people are unaware of this fact.  *Appropriately* and *deftly* are wrong for the first blank, and *abhorrent* or *disconnected* do not make sense in the second.

| Slot | Option | Meaning and evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| **Blank (i)** | **appropriately** | In a suitable manner; calling a peanut a nut is not appropriate. |
|  | **erroneously** | In a way that is wrong or false【525914757144305†L61-L65】; accurately describes mislabeling. **Correct.** |
|  | **deftly** | Skillfully or adroitly; this describes manner of action, not correctness, so it doesn’t fit. |
| **Blank (ii)** | **unbeknownst** | Without someone’s knowledge【870173287945705†L73-L75】; many people do not know peanuts are legumes. **Correct.** |
|  | **abhorrent** | Causing disgust or loathing; irrelevant to the botanical fact. |
|  | **disconnected** | Separate or unlinked; again unrelated to knowledge. |

## Q59

**Answer:** **(i) verdant; (ii) castigated**

**Reasoning:** The sentence refers to a **young employee** who made a costly mistake and was promptly reprimanded.  **Verdant** can mean covered in green but in figurative usage it also means inexperienced【965539723864860†L30-L42】, exactly the sense of “green” as “new.”  The verb **castigate** means to criticize severely【505657898390070†L61-L64】.  Thus the inexperienced employee was castigated.  Other options either do not describe inexperience or do not convey harsh criticism.

| Slot | Option | Meaning and evaluation |
|---|---|---|

|  | **sagacious** | Having or showing wisdom; again, opposite of inexperienced. |
|  | **verdant** | Figuratively means unsophisticated or inexperienced【965539723864860†L30-L42】; apt description of a young novice. **Correct.** |

|  | **disturbed** | Upset or troubled; the subject here is how the supervisor reacted, not the employee’s emotional state. |

## Q60

**Answer:** **(i) benign; (ii) venturesome; (iii) accessible**

**Reasoning:** The passage predicts positive outcomes from electronic books.  Their effect on young readers will be **benign**—harmless or beneficial【319048105532343†L86-L100】—rather than deleterious (harmful) or startling (shocking).  E‑books make reading interactive and engaging, encouraging children to be more **venturesome** (willing to take risks or explore)【142856505913184†L83-L92】 with their reading selections rather than indolent (lazy) or backward (behind).  Finally, parents will purchase educational books that were not previously **accessible**; electronic formats make materials easier to obtain.  “Sanctioned” (authorized) and “benevolent” (well‑meaning) do not fit this sense.

| Slot | Option | Meaning and evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| **Blank (i)** | **startling** | Causing surprise; the effect described is expected to be positive and non‑disruptive. |
|  | **benign** | Harmless or favorable【319048105532343†L86-L100】; describes a good effect on young readers. **Correct.** |
|  | **deleterious** | Harmful; the passage anticipates the opposite. |
