# Text Completion Explanations: Questions 71–80

This section continues the detailed analysis of the GRE Text Completion questions.  For each item, the correct answer is explained in context, and every answer option is defined and contrasted.  Dictionary definitions are cited where appropriate to justify word meanings.

## Q71

**Correct Answer:** *insurmountable*

**Reasoning:**  The question describes how Roger Bannister’s breaking of the four‑minute mile inspired others to run faster.  The earlier “hurdles” were thought to be impossible, yet Bannister’s success encouraged athletes to tackle these seemingly **insurmountable** barriers.  Collins English Dictionary defines an *insurmountable* problem as one “so great that it cannot be dealt with successfully”【128611018565592†L213-L216】.  Only this word captures the notion of an obstacle previously believed impossible to overcome.

**Option analysis:**

| Option | Meaning | Why it’s wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **insurmountable** | So great it cannot be overcome【128611018565592†L213-L216】. | Correct – matches the idea of barriers once believed unbeatable. |
| **improbable** | Not likely to happen; improbable events are unlikely. | The hurdles were not merely unlikely; they were regarded as impossible until Bannister showed otherwise. |
| **trivial** | Of little value or importance. | The context implies very serious obstacles, not unimportant ones. |
| **definable** | Able to be defined or described. | Being hard to define has nothing to do with running records. |
| **negligible** | So small or unimportant as to be not worth considering. | The opposite of the giant hurdles described. |

## Q72

**Correct Answer:** *impecunious*

**Reasoning:**  The passage notes that the woman later embellished her “humble beginnings,” implying she had not truly been poor because she possessed considerable wealth.  The adjective **impecunious** means “having very little or no money; penniless”【292933127465673†L85-L87】.  Thus she could not claim to have been impecunious.  The contrast makes *impecunious* the correct choice.

**Option analysis:**

| Option | Meaning | Why it’s wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **impecunious** | Having little or no money; penniless【292933127465673†L85-L87】. | Correct – the woman was *not* impecunious because she was wealthy. |
| **indefatigable** | Incapable of being fatigued; untiring【785223321377819†L84-L86】. | Irrelevant to wealth or humble beginnings. |
| **resplendent** | Shining brilliantly or richly colorful. | Concerns appearance, not financial status. |
| **nonchalant** | Feeling or appearing casually calm and relaxed. | Unrelated to the claim of poverty. |
| **admirable** | Deserving respect or approval【72530144677082†L213-L218】. | Does not address the issue of poverty versus wealth. |

## Q73

**Correct Answer:** *(i) adroit; (ii) looming*

**Reasoning:**  In this sentence, a politician’s “handling” of an unfolding scandal prevented his coalition from disintegrating.  The first blank must denote skill: **adroit** means “having or showing skill, cleverness, or resourcefulness”【735046005977445†L58-L63】.  The second blank should describe the scandal as impending; *looming* means “imminent, about to happen soon and causing worry”【482133731600100†L34-L44】.  An “adroit” leader dealing with a “looming” scandal fits perfectly.

**Option analysis:**

| Blank | Option | Meaning | Why it’s wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(i)** | **adroit** | Skillful, clever, resourceful【735046005977445†L58-L63】 | Correct – describes the skilled handling. |
|  | **clumsy** | Awkward, lacking skill. | Contradicts the effective leadership described. |
|  | **obstinate** | Stubbornly refusing to change. | Does not imply competence. |
| **(ii)** | **looming** | Imminent; about to happen and often threatening【482133731600100†L34-L44】 | Correct – the scandal was approaching. |
|  | **distant** | Far off in time or place. | Does not capture urgency. |
|  | **abating** | Becoming less intense; diminishing. | Opposite of the escalating scandal. |

## Q74

**Correct Answer:** *(i) frugal; (ii) delectable*

**Reasoning:**  The statement contrasts a person’s attempt to live modestly with indulgence in fine food.  **Frugal** means “characterized by or reflecting thrift and efficiency in the use of resources”【601586545305364†L88-L90】—living simply and avoiding excess.  **Delectable** is defined as “highly pleasing; delightful; delicious”【264331410544103†L90-L103】, describing luxurious dishes.  So the sentence means the person was frugal but still enjoyed delectable meals.

**Option analysis:**

| Blank | Option | Meaning | Why it’s wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(i)** | **frugal** | Thrifty; economical in using resources【601586545305364†L88-L90】 | Correct – captures living economically. |
|  | **ostentatious** | Showy; intended to attract notice. | Opposite of modest spending. |
|  | **apathetic** | Showing or feeling no interest. | Does not fit the context of lifestyle. |
| **(ii)** | **delectable** | Highly pleasing or delicious【264331410544103†L90-L103】 | Correct – describes indulgent, delicious food. |
|  | **insipid** | Lacking flavor; dull. | Contradicts the appeal of the food. |
|  | **rigid** | Stiff; inflexible. | Unrelated to food quality. |

## Q75

**Correct Answer:** *(i) provincial; (ii) kept abreast of*

**Reasoning:**  The sentence remarks that staying at home did not make the artist narrow‑minded; he remained informed about developments in the wider world.  **Provincial** refers to having the narrow attitudes of unsophisticated inhabitants of a province【500126375049427†L38-L41】.  To “keep abreast of” something means to remain current or up to date.  Hence the artist was not provincial because he kept abreast of world art.

**Option analysis:**

| Blank | Option | Meaning | Why it’s wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(i)** | **provincial** | Having the viewpoints of unsophisticated rural inhabitants; narrow or parochial【500126375049427†L38-L41】 | Correct – the negative quality he avoided. |
|  | **cosmopolitan** | Sophisticated and worldly. | Contradicts the criticism implied. |
|  | **upstart** | A person of humble origin who has risen suddenly; presumptuous. | Not relevant. |
| **(ii)** | **kept abreast of** | Remained aware of or up to date. | Correct – explains why he wasn’t provincial. |
|  | **turned away from** | Rejected or refused. | Contradicts his engagement with contemporary art. |
|  | **fell behind** | Failed to keep pace. | Opposite of remaining up to date. |

## Q76

**Correct Answer:** *cordiality*

**Reasoning:**  The passage compares attitudes toward neighbors in England and America, noting that in the U.S. residential friendliness matters a lot.  The desired noun should capture warm and hearty friendliness.  **Cordial** describes something “showing or marked by warm and often hearty friendliness”【726060586112419†L94-L102】; from that root, **cordiality** means genuine warmth and sincerity in relations.  Therefore, the correct answer is *cordiality*.

**Option analysis:**

| Option | Meaning | Why it’s wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **cordiality** | Warm, hearty friendliness【726060586112419†L94-L102】. | Correct – captures neighborly warmth valued in the U.S. |
| **antagonism** | Active hostility or opposition. | Opposite of friendliness. |
| **aloofness** | Emotional distance; indifference. | Does not fit a culture that values social warmth. |
| **misery** | Great distress or unhappiness. | Unrelated to social relations. |
| **sophistication** | Refinement and worldly experience. | Concerns manners but not specifically friendliness. |

## Q77

**Correct Answer:** *truculence*

**Reasoning:**  The Prussians’ reputation for fierceness on the battlefield suggests the answer should denote aggressiveness.  **Truculence** is “the quality or attitude of being aggressively hostile”【851927735973470†L27-L31】.  This perfectly matches a fearsome reputation.  Other options like *docility* (gentleness) or *magnanimity* (generosity) do not convey this belligerent trait.

**Option analysis:**

| Option | Meaning | Why it’s wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **truculence** | Aggressive hostility; belligerence【851927735973470†L27-L31】. | Correct – describes Prussian fierceness. |
| **docility** | Submissiveness; willingness to be taught. | Opposite of being fearsome. |
| **magnanimity** | Generosity or nobility of spirit. | Inconsistent with a reputation for militaristic ferocity. |
| **acuity** | Sharpness of thought or vision. | Describes perception, not aggression. |
| **ingenuity** | Inventiveness or cleverness. | Not related to martial aggression. |

## Q78

**Correct Answer:** *(i) recidivism; (ii) ramifications*

**Reasoning:**  The passage states that restorative justice decreases the tendency of offenders to repeat crimes because facing victims helps them grasp the consequences of their actions.  **Recidivism** means a tendency to relapse into a previous condition, especially criminal behaviour【76625614544446†L78-L83】.  **Ramifications** refers to consequences or outgrowths【177084914638983†L85-L111】.  Offenders who appreciate the ramifications of their crimes become less likely to reoffend, so the two blanks must be filled by these words.

**Option analysis:**

| Blank | Option | Meaning | Why it’s wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(i)** | **recidivism** | A tendency to relapse, especially into criminal behaviour【76625614544446†L78-L83】 | Correct – describes repeat offending. |
|  | **rehabilitation** | The process of restoring to normal life. | Opposite of reoffending. |
|  | **legislation** | Laws or the act of making laws. | Irrelevant to reoffending. |
| **(ii)** | **ramifications** | Consequences or outgrowths【177084914638983†L85-L111】 | Correct – offenders face the real impacts of their crimes. |
|  | **precedents** | Previous cases that guide later decisions. | Does not convey personal consequences. |
|  | **credentials** | Qualifications or achievements. | Not related to crime and punishment. |

## Q79

**Correct Answer:** *innocuous*

**Reasoning:**  The question contrasts mild stimuli with mildly painful ones; it notes that harmless distractions provide less relief.  **Innocuous** means “not likely to offend or cause injury; harmless”【896152037524207†L84-L102】.  The other options imply harm (*deleterious*), startling effect (*startling*), or boring content (*banal*).  Thus *innocuous* best contrasts with painful stimuli while remaining consistent with the context.

**Option analysis:**

| Option | Meaning | Why it’s wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **innocuous** | Harmless; not likely to offend【896152037524207†L84-L102】. | Correct – describes mild, harmless stimuli. |
| **deleterious** | Harmful or damaging. | Opposite of harmless. |
| **banal** | So lacking in originality as to be obvious and boring. | Irrelevant to the harmless‑versus‑painful contrast. |
| **startling** | Very surprising or astonishing. | Describes shocking stimuli, not mild. |
| **mendacious** | Habitually dishonest【549522828820671†L28-L34】. | Unconnected to physical sensations. |

## Q80

**Correct Answer:** *(i) derivation; (ii) notorious; (iii) opacity*

**Reasoning:**  The sentence discusses the origin of a word and how scholars debate the obscure origins of an assassins’ sect.  The first blank should reference “the origin of a word”: **derivation** in linguistics is “the formation of a word from another word or base” and, by extension, the word’s source【39785393489444†L84-L114】.  The second blank needs a term meaning widely known (often for something bad); *notorious* describes being well known for a bad reason【748452284017964†L213-L216】.  The final blank must denote obscurity or lack of clarity: **opacity** refers to “obscurity of sense” or a quality of being difficult to understand【885325055151689†L88-L104】.  Together, these fill all blanks coherently: philologists debate the derivation of *assassin*, its origin is notorious among philologists, and the opacity of the sect’s history invites research.

**Option analysis:**

| Blank | Option | Meaning | Why it’s wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(i)** | **derivation** | In linguistics, the formation of a word from another word; the source or origin of a word【39785393489444†L84-L114】 | Correct – fits “origin” of the word *assassin*. |
|  | **etymology** | The study of word origins. | Close, but the sentence contrasts the specific origin of *assassin* with the general study; *derivation* better suits the structure. |
|  | **propagation** | The act of spreading or reproducing. | Unrelated to word origins. |
| **(ii)** | **notorious** | Well known for something bad【748452284017964†L213-L216】 | Correct – the word’s origin is well known among scholars for its shocking associations. |
|  | **celebrated** | Widely admired or praised【917765539874742†L83-L85】 | Suggests positive fame, which doesn’t fit a murderous sect. |
|  | **obscure** | Not clearly expressed; hard to understand. | Would imply the origin is unknown, but the sentence treats the word’s derivation as well known among scholars. |
| **(iii)** | **opacity** | Obscurity of sense; unintelligibility; lack of transparency【885325055151689†L88-L104】 | Correct – describes the mysterious history of the sect. |
|  | **simplicity** | The quality of being simple. | Opposite of the complexity described. |
|  | **plausibility** | The quality of seeming reasonable or probable. | Does not convey the mystery in the sect’s history. |
