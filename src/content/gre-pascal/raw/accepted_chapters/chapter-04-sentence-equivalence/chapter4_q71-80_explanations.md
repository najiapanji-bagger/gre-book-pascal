# Chapter 4 – Sentence Equivalence Questions 71‑80

The following explanations continue the analysis of the GRE Pascal book’s Sentence Equivalence exercises.  Each question asks you to pick two answer choices that produce equivalent meanings in the blank.  For each item, I summarize the context, identify the correct pair, and briefly explain why the other options are less suitable.  A small table accompanies each explanation to clarify the meanings of the key words.

## Q71

**Prompt.** French cultural subsidies allow producers of nearly any film to secure advance funding from the government, even though such loans are rarely repaid.  This implies that subsidies are widespread rather than rare or questionable.

**Correct pair: *ubiquitous* and *omnipresent*.** The best pair conveys that subsidies are available everywhere and to everyone.  *Ubiquitous* means “found or existing everywhere”【749955949858773†L61-L65】, and *omnipresent* similarly means “present in all places at all times”【605447421127426†L81-L83】.  No other options share this meaning.  *Invaluable* suggests great worth, while *sporadic* and *scanty* mean infrequent or meager.  *Questionable* implies dubious legitimacy, which doesn’t fit.

**Keyword meanings**

| Keyword | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **ubiquitous** | seeming to be everywhere; found or existing everywhere【749955949858773†L61-L65】 |
| **omnipresent** | present in all places at all times【605447421127426†L81-L83】 |

## Q72

**Prompt.** A politician pretends to care about constituents, but his concern is so slight as to be practically nonexistent.

**Correct pair: *negligible* and *flimsy*.** Both words describe something insubstantial.  *Negligible* means “too slight or small in amount to be of importance”【601982315351258†L61-L63】.  *Flimsy*, when applied to an excuse or claim, means “not very good or convincing”【294198139560589†L234-L239】.  Thus his concern is so minimal that it doesn’t warrant attention.  *Profound* and *exemplary* suggest depth or excellence; *abundant* implies plenty.  *Moribund* means near death, which doesn’t fit.

**Keyword meanings**

| Keyword | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **negligible** | too slight or small to be important【601982315351258†L61-L63】 |
| **flimsy** | weak or unconvincing (e.g., a flimsy excuse)【294198139560589†L234-L239】 |

## Q73

**Prompt.** A novel of nearly 450 pages is full of unnecessary flourishes; the author rarely resists adding extra length.

**Correct pair: *prolix* and *long‑winded*.** These words suggest excessive length.  *Prolix* describes writing that uses too many words and is therefore boring【841270329561438†L61-L65】.  *Long‑winded* refers to speech or writing that is too long or uses too many words【212661082052385†L59-L60】.  The other options do not fit: *instructive* and *educational* suggest utility, while *complex* and *explicit* describe content rather than length.

**Keyword meanings**

| Keyword | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **prolix** | using too many words and therefore boring or difficult to read【841270329561438†L61-L65】 |
| **long‑winded** | too long or using too many words【212661082052385†L59-L60】 |

## Q74

**Prompt.** An editorial criticizes a gubernatorial candidate’s lack of worldly wisdom and predicts that this flaw will doom his campaign.

**Correct pair: *naïveté* and *gullibility*.** The editorial suggests the candidate’s lack of sophistication.  *Naivety* is “trust based on not having much experience”【597796919541511†L61-L66】.  *Gullible* people are “easily deceived or tricked and too willing to believe everything others say”【572829645557343†L59-L63】.  Therefore, his naïveté/gullibility will cause problems.  Other choices such as *furtiveness* (secretiveness), *venality* (susceptibility to bribery), and *indecisiveness* or *slyness* do not match the idea of innocence.

**Keyword meanings**

| Keyword | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **naïveté** | trust or innocence resulting from a lack of experience【597796919541511†L61-L66】 |
| **gullibility** | being easily deceived or too willing to believe others【572829645557343†L59-L63】 |

## Q75

**Prompt.** Analysts worry that consumers perceive the electronics industry as always nearing major breakthroughs, causing them to delay purchases lest current products soon become outdated.

**Correct pair: *obsolete* and *outmoded*.** Both words mean outdated.  *Obsolete* refers to something “not in use anymore, having been replaced by something newer and better”【845445232139271†L61-L65】.  *Outmoded* means “no longer modern, useful, or necessary”【872987025758126†L61-L63】.  No other pair conveys the fear that products will quickly lose relevance; *incompatible* and *devalued* miss the nuance, while *ubiquitous* and *everywhere* are unrelated.

**Keyword meanings**

| Keyword | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **obsolete** | no longer used because something newer has replaced it【845445232139271†L61-L65】 |
| **outmoded** | no longer modern, useful, or necessary【872987025758126†L61-L63】 |

## Q76

## Q77

**Prompt.** In his popular book *The Simple Soybean*, the author praises soy’s medical efficacy but cautions against treating it as a universal remedy.

**Correct pair: *a panacea* and *a cure‑all*.** Both terms refer to a supposed remedy for every problem.  A *panacea* is “something that will solve all problems or cure all illnesses”【50519672926719†L59-L64】.  A *cure‑all* is “something that people think will solve any problem or cure any illness”【532384098492047†L59-L63】.  Other options such as *a staple*, *a supplement*, or *an antidote* describe legitimate uses, whereas the author warns against viewing soy in such exalted, unfounded terms.

**Keyword meanings**

| Keyword | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **panacea** | something that will solve all problems or cure all illnesses【50519672926719†L59-L64】 |
| **cure‑all** | something believed to solve any problem or cure any illness【532384098492047†L59-L63】 |

## Q78

**Prompt.** Parkin’s description of a movement as “neo‑scholastic” is too superficial to be accepted without further investigation.

**Correct pair: *cursory* and *perfunctory*.** Both words indicate a lack of depth.  *Cursory* means “quick and probably not detailed”【37792163322623†L59-L63】.  *Perfunctory* describes something “done quickly and without showing care or much interest”【319686416023094†L59-L61】.  Therefore, Parkin’s analysis is superficial.  The other options do not pair to express superficiality; *biased* and *prejudiced* suggest unfairness, while *detailed* and *self‑evident* are opposite in meaning.

**Keyword meanings**

| Keyword | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **cursory** | quick and not detailed【37792163322623†L59-L63】 |
| **perfunctory** | done quickly and without care or interest【319686416023094†L59-L61】 |

## Q79

**Prompt.** A study shows that vitamin E supplements, despite widespread belief in their effectiveness, are no better than sugar pills at delaying a degenerative disease.

**Correct pair: *efficacy* and *usefulness*.** The belief at issue concerns the supplements’ ability to produce a desired effect.  *Efficacy* refers to “the ability of something to produce the intended result”【195237172090512†L61-L64】.  *Usefulness* is “the quality or state of being useful”【57984379436729†L61-L64】.  *Popularity* and *prevalence* refer to how common a product is, not its effectiveness; *occurrence* and *misuse* are likewise irrelevant.

**Keyword meanings**

| Keyword | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **efficacy** | ability of something to produce the intended result【195237172090512†L61-L64】 |
| **usefulness** | quality or state of being useful【57984379436729†L61-L64】 |

## Q80

These adjectives highlight her business acumen.  The remaining options—*ruthless*, *creative*, *industrious*, and *effective*—do not capture this specific combination of savvy and judgment.

**Keyword meanings**

| Keyword | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **canny** | thinking quickly and cleverly, especially in business【57147915663966†L61-L64】 |
