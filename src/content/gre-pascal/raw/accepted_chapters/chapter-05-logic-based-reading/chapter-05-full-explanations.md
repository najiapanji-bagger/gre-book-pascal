## Question 1

### Source
- **Source page:** page 073 (`extracted_images/images/page_073.jpg`)
- **Chapter:** 5 — Logic‑Based Reading Comprehension
- **Type:** additional facts

### Prompt / Passage
Total contributions by individuals to political parties were up **25 percent** in this most recent presidential election over those of four years earlier.  Hence, it is obvious that people are no longer as **apathetic** as they were, but are taking a greater interest in politics.

### Question Stem
Which of the following, if true, would **considerably weaken** the preceding argument?

### Choices
A. The average contribution per individual actually declined during the same four‑year period.  
B. Per capita income of the population increased by **15 percent** during the four years in question.  
C. Public leaders continue to warn citizens against the dangers of political apathy.  
D. Contributions made by large corporations to political parties declined during the four‑year period.  
E. **Fewer people voted** in the most recent presidential election than in the one four years earlier.

### Correct Answer
**E** — Fewer people voted in the most recent presidential election than in the one four years earlier.

### Argument Map
- **Premise:** Total contributions by individuals to political parties increased by 25 percent compared with the previous election.
- **Conclusion:** People are less apathetic and more interested in politics now than they were before.
- **Assumption:** Greater donations reflect broader political engagement; that is, rising contributions indicate that more people are participating in politics rather than a small group giving more money.
- **Logical gap:** The argument conflates the **amount** of money donated with the **number of donors**.  Donations could rise even if fewer people participate (for example, if a few wealthy individuals give more), so increased contributions do not necessarily indicate decreased apathy.
- **Structure:** Inductive reasoning based on observed data (higher donations → conclusion about voter engagement).

### Question Clues / Keywords
| Keyword / phrase from question | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **apathetic** | Lacking interest, concern or emotion; showing little or no feeling of involvement【986553967137448†L84-L89】 | The conclusion claims people are “no longer as apathetic,” so understanding that apathy relates to engagement clarifies that the argument is about overall political participation, not just monetary giving. |
| **25 percent increase in contributions** | A quantitative change in donations compared with the previous election | Highlights that the premise refers to money, not necessarily to the number of participants; this sets up the possibility that the same donors simply gave more. |
| **Fewer people voted** | Indicates voter turnout was lower than before | If turnout declined, it undermines the claim that people are less apathetic despite the higher donation total. |

### Logic
The argument assumes a direct correlation between the total amount of donations and overall public engagement.  To weaken the argument, we need evidence showing that increased donations do not reflect increased participation.  Option E states that **fewer people voted** in the most recent election.  If turnout fell, the increased contributions could be the result of a smaller number of donors giving more money, which would undermine the conclusion that people are less apathetic.

### Why the Correct Answer Works
If fewer people vote despite higher contributions, the supposed decline in apathy is called into question.  The increase in donations could come from a handful of enthusiastic donors rather than a broader shift in public interest.  Therefore, voter apathy may actually have grown even though donations increased, making option E the most damaging to the argument.

### Why Other Options Are Wrong
- **A.** Showing that the **average** contribution declined is compatible with more people giving small donations; this does not necessarily weaken the link between donations and participation.
- **B.** Higher per‑capita income could allow people to donate more; it does not challenge the premise that increased donations imply greater interest.
- **C.** Public warnings about political apathy do not address whether apathy has decreased; such warnings can continue even when interest rises.
- **D.** Declining corporate donations imply that individual donations may make up a larger share of the total; this could strengthen the argument by suggesting that individuals (not corporations) are giving more.

### Final Answer
E

## Question 2

### Source
- **Source page:** page 073 (`extracted_images/images/page_073.jpg`)
- **Chapter:** 5 — Logic‑Based Reading Comprehension
- **Type:** additional facts

### Prompt / Passage
Animal‑rights supporters in the United States have, in the last **two years**, increased their activities protesting the use of animals for furs.  During this period **fur coat sales began to decrease steadily**.  Therefore, the activities of the animal‑rights supporters must have been the cause of the decline in fur sales.

### Question Stem
Which of the following, if true, would be **most damaging** to the argument above?

### Choices
A. In the past two years, production problems have restricted the supply of furs, making each fur coat more expensive.  
B. Fur coats are very warm, and in the past two years several northern regions in the United States have experienced record cold winters, causing an increase in the need for warm outer wear.  
C. In the past several years, fur‑industry analysts have noted that middle‑income consumers are buying more fur coats than previously.  
D. In the past few years, protests by animal‑rights supporters have caused many celebrities to speak out against the fur industry.  
E. At the recent fur fashion shows, many of the furs were sheared and dyed bright colours so that they resembled velvet rather than fur.

### Correct Answer
**A** — In the past two years, production problems have restricted the supply of furs, making each fur coat more expensive.

### Argument Map
- **Premise:** Animal‑rights protests increased; during the same period fur‑coat sales declined steadily.
- **Conclusion:** The protests caused the decline in fur‑coat sales.
- **Assumption:** Nothing else changed significantly that could explain the decrease in fur‑coat sales; therefore the decline must be due to the protests.
- **Logical gap:** Correlation does not imply causation.  A simultaneous decline in sales may be due to other factors such as supply restrictions or fashion trends.
- **Structure:** Causal inference drawn from a temporal correlation.

### Question Clues / Keywords
| Keyword / phrase | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **increased … activities** | The protests intensified during the last two years | Suggests a temporal link between protests and sales decline; this connection needs to be tested. |
| **fur coat sales decreased steadily** | Observed outcome during the same period | The argument assumes the protests caused this outcome. |
| **production problems** | Difficulties that restrict supply | If supply problems raise prices, sales could fall for reasons unrelated to protests; thus this fact would undermine the causal link. |

### Logic
The argument connects two events (increased protests and declining sales) and assumes a causal relationship.  To undermine this connection we must introduce an alternative explanation for the decline.  Option A points to **production problems** that restrict the supply of furs and raise prices.  If coats become scarce and more expensive, sales could drop independently of protest activities.  Therefore, this fact severely weakens the argument by providing another plausible cause.

### Why the Correct Answer Works
Production problems that make fur coats more expensive would naturally reduce sales.  If the decline can be explained by supply and price issues, the protests are not necessarily the cause.  Because the argument depends on a single cause, introducing a credible alternative cause destroys its validity.

### Why Other Options Are Wrong
- **B.** Record cold winters increasing the need for warm coats would, if anything, raise demand for fur coats.  This fact might contradict the observed decline but does not weaken the causal argument.
- **C.** Increased purchases by middle‑income consumers would tend to increase sales, not decrease them; it conflicts with the premise rather than offering an alternative cause.
- **D.** Celebrity criticism of the fur industry is similar to the activists’ protests; it reinforces the argument rather than undermining it.
- **E.** Changes in fashion such as shearing and dyeing furs may influence consumer preferences, but the fact that they resemble velvet does not directly explain a decline in sales and thus is less damaging than a supply shortage.

### Final Answer
A

### Prompt / Passage
The **percentage of patients** who die while being treated at **Hospital M** is almost **twice** as large as the percentage of patients who die while being treated at **Hospital R**.  Therefore, the doctors at Hospital R are **more skilled** than those at Hospital M.

### Question Stem
Which of the following, if true, **most seriously weakens** the conclusion of the argument above?

### Choices
A. Doctors at Hospital M, on average, have as many years of experience at practicing medicine in a hospital setting as those at Hospital R.  
B. The ratio of doctors to patients at Hospital M is greater than the ratio of doctors to patients at Hospital R.  
C. Hospital M specialises in **acute cardiovascular issues** and treats a much higher percentage of **acutely ill patients** than does Hospital R.  
D. The county health department has reprimanded Hospital R for violations of the local health code that Hospital M has not committed.  
E. The average length of stay for patients at Hospital M is significantly longer than the average length of stay for patients at Hospital R.

### Correct Answer
**C** — Hospital M specialises in acute cardiovascular issues and treats a much higher percentage of acutely ill patients than does Hospital R.

### Argument Map
- **Premise:** A larger percentage of patients die at Hospital M than at Hospital R.
- **Conclusion:** Doctors at Hospital R are more skilled than those at Hospital M.
- **Assumption:** The death rate is solely or primarily a function of physician skill; the patient populations are comparable in severity and hospital conditions are similar.
- **Logical gap:** The argument fails to consider differences in case mix.  Hospitals may treat patients of differing severity; higher mortality may result from treating sicker patients rather than lower physician skill.
- **Structure:** Statistical comparison used to infer causal competence.

### Question Clues / Keywords
| Keyword / phrase | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **percentage of patients who die** | Mortality rate | The argument relies on raw mortality rates as a proxy for physician skill. |
| **acute cardiovascular issues** | Serious heart‑related conditions | If Hospital M treats sicker patients (e.g., heart attacks), mortality will naturally be higher, weakening the skill‑based comparison. |
| **more skilled** | Having superior ability or expertise | The conclusion equates lower death rates with better skill, ignoring other factors. |

### Logic
The argument assumes comparability between the two hospitals.  A high death rate does not necessarily mean poor medical skill; it may reflect a hospital’s role in treating more serious cases.  Option C states that Hospital M specialises in acute cardiovascular issues and treats a larger proportion of critically ill patients.  This alternative explanation undermines the inference that doctors at Hospital R are more skilled.  If Hospital M handles more difficult cases, its higher mortality rate is expected and does not reflect inferior care.

### Why the Correct Answer Works
Case‑mix differences explain the mortality disparity without attributing it to skill.  When patient populations differ significantly, mortality comparisons are not valid measures of physician competence.  Option C directly addresses this by showing that Hospital M’s case mix is more severe; thus the conclusion that Hospital R’s doctors are more skilled collapses.

### Why Other Options Are Wrong
- **A.** Similar years of experience do not challenge the reasoning; the argument does not hinge on experience but on outcomes.
- **B.** A higher doctor‑to‑patient ratio at Hospital M might lead to better care, not worse; it does not explain the mortality gap.
- **D.** Health code violations at Hospital R might undermine its safety but do not account for mortality differences; this option does not weaken the claim that Hospital R’s doctors are more skilled.
- **E.** A longer average stay at Hospital M might indicate more extensive care or more serious conditions; it does not directly challenge the skill‑based conclusion unless connected to case severity.

### Final Answer
C

### Prompt / Passage
Many people ask, **“How effective is PainAway?”**  So, to find out we have been checking the medicine cabinets of the apartments in this building.  As it turns out, **eight out of ten** contain a bottle of PainAway.  Doesn’t it stand to reason that you, too, should have the most effective pain reliever on the market?

### Question Stem
The appeal of this advertisement would be **most weakened** by which of the following pieces of evidence?

### Choices
A. PainAway distributed complimentary bottles of medicine to most apartments in the building two days before the advertisement aired.  
B. The actor who is featured in the advertisement takes a pain reliever manufactured by a competitor of PainAway.  
C. Most people want a fast, effective pain reliever.  
D. Many people take the advice of their doctors rather than that of commercials about pain relievers.  
E. A government survey shows that many people take a pain reliever before it is really needed.

### Correct Answer
**A** — PainAway distributed complimentary bottles of medicine to most apartments in the building two days before the advertisement aired.

### Argument Map
- **Premise:** Inspectors found that **eight out of ten** apartments in a building contained a bottle of PainAway.
- **Conclusion:** PainAway must be effective; therefore you should have it too.
- **Assumption:** The presence of PainAway in most apartments indicates voluntary choice based on the product’s effectiveness, rather than external factors such as free samples.
- **Logical gap:** Ownership of a product does not necessarily reflect its effectiveness; distribution methods (e.g., free giveaways) can inflate its presence.
- **Structure:** Appeal to popularity (bandwagon) used to infer quality.

### Question Clues / Keywords
| Keyword / phrase | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **eight out of ten contain** | 80 % of apartments have PainAway | Suggests wide usage, but does not explain why the product is present. |
| **complimentary bottles** | Free samples distributed to potential customers | If most bottles were given away, their presence does not signal consumer preference or product effectiveness. |
| **most effective pain reliever** | Claim that the product’s prevalence proves its quality | Assumes usage equates to efficacy. |

### Logic
The advertisement relies on a popularity appeal: because many people possess PainAway, it must be effective.  To weaken this argument we need evidence that the high presence of PainAway is not due to consumer choice.  Option A reveals that PainAway distributed free bottles to most apartments just before the survey.  If the product was given away, its prevalence does not reflect perceived effectiveness, undermining the argument’s premise.

### Why the Correct Answer Works
By showing that PainAway’s bottles were distributed as complimentary samples, option A demonstrates that their widespread presence is not evidence of effectiveness or consumer preference.  The high ownership rate could simply reflect a marketing tactic, so the advertisement’s conclusion is invalid.

### Why Other Options Are Wrong
- **B.** The actor’s personal preference for a competitor’s product is irrelevant to whether PainAway is effective for consumers in general.
- **C.** Stating that most people want a fast, effective pain reliever does not address whether PainAway meets that need or why it is widely present.
- **D.** The fact that people often listen to doctors rather than commercials weakens advertising effectiveness in general but does not specifically explain the high presence of PainAway.
- **E.** People taking pain relievers when unnecessary indicates overuse; it does not undermine the argument that PainAway’s prevalence signals effectiveness.

### Final Answer
A

### Prompt / Passage
Of the graduating students from **Governor Smith Academy**, a private high school, **93 percent** go on to college.  From **Eastern High**, the public high school in the same city, **only 74 percent** go on to attend college.  As a result, many parents with children about to enter high school believe that **Governor Smith Academy gives students a better education** than they can get at Eastern High School.

### Question Stem
The conclusion of these parents is **most weakened** by which of the following additional facts?

### Choices
A. Until 1992, Governor Smith Academy was exclusively a girls’ school, but Eastern High School has always been coeducational.  
B. Governor Smith Academy requires students to pass an **admissions examination** before entering, but Eastern High School admits all applicants who live in the city.  
C. Eastern High School has problems with severe student violence during school hours.  
D. Governor Smith Academy has a higher percentage of students attending Ivy League colleges than any other high school in the state.  
E. Eastern High School receives its funding from local property taxes, while Governor Smith Academy receives funding from tuition costs and from alumni donations.

### Correct Answer
**B** — Governor Smith Academy requires students to pass an admissions examination before entering, but Eastern High School admits all applicants who live in the city.

### Argument Map
- **Premise:** A private school (Governor Smith Academy) sends 93 % of its students to college; a public school (Eastern High) sends only 74 %.
- **Conclusion:** Governor Smith Academy provides a better education than Eastern High.
- **Assumption:** The difference in college attendance rates reflects the quality of education rather than differences in student populations.
- **Logical gap:** Private schools may select students through admissions criteria; public schools must accept all local applicants.  Student selection, not teaching quality, may account for higher college attendance rates.
- **Structure:** Comparative argument; it infers quality from outcomes without controlling for input differences.

### Question Clues / Keywords
| Keyword / phrase | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **93 percent vs 74 percent** | Difference in college attendance rates | The raw statistics motivate the parents’ conclusion. |
| **admissions examination** | Selective entry requirement at Governor Smith Academy | Suggests that the private school screens applicants and thus starts with students more likely to attend college. |
| **admits all applicants** | Eastern High’s open‑admissions policy | Indicates that the public school has a more varied student body and may start with students less likely to attend college. |

### Logic
To weaken the parents’ conclusion, we need to show that the higher college attendance rate at Governor Smith Academy could result from factors other than teaching quality.  Option B identifies a key difference: the private school requires an admissions exam, whereas the public school accepts all students.  If Governor Smith Academy selectively admits academically strong or motivated students, those students are more likely to attend college regardless of the school’s instruction.  This undermines the conclusion that the private school provides a superior education.

### Why the Correct Answer Works
By highlighting the difference in admissions policies, option B shows that the two schools are not comparable.  The private school may simply enroll students who are predisposed to attend college.  The outcome (college attendance) therefore cannot be attributed solely to the quality of the education provided.

### Why Other Options Are Wrong
- **A.** The historical gender composition of Governor Smith Academy does not directly affect the current comparison.
- **C.** Problems with violence at Eastern High may deter students or affect learning, but the question asks for the fact that weakens the conclusion that Governor Smith Academy provides a better education.  Violence at Eastern may strengthen, not weaken, the claim.
- **D.** The percentage of students attending Ivy League colleges is consistent with high student quality; it could reinforce the parents’ conclusion rather than weaken it.
- **E.** Funding sources differ, but the impact on educational quality is ambiguous; it does not directly address differences in student populations or outcomes.

### Final Answer
B

### Prompt / Passage
Using sophisticated software‑based technologies, researchers are now able to analyse layers of paint that lie buried beneath the surface of old paintings.  These researchers claim, for example, that **additional mountainous scenery once appeared in Leonardo da Vinci’s *Mona Lisa***, but that this scenery was later painted over.  **Skeptics** reply to these claims, however, that **X‑ray examinations of the Mona Lisa do not show hidden mountains**.

### Question Stem
Which of the following, if true, would tend most to **weaken the force** of the skeptics’ objections?

### Choices
A. There is no written or anecdotal record that Leonardo da Vinci ever painted over major areas of his *Mona Lisa*.  
B. Painters of da Vinci’s time commonly created images of mountainous backgrounds within portraits like the *Mona Lisa*.  
C. No one knows for certain which parts of the *Mona Lisa* may have been painted by da Vinci’s assistants rather than by da Vinci himself.  
D. In addition to X‑ray examinations, infrared photography of the *Mona Lisa* has also not revealed any trace of hidden mountainous scenery.  
E. Analysis relying on **X‑ray only has the capacity to detect lead‑based white pigments** in layers of paint beneath a painting’s surface layers.

### Correct Answer
**E** — Analysis relying on X‑ray only has the capacity to detect lead‑based white pigments in layers of paint beneath a painting’s surface layers.

### Argument Map
- **Premise:** Researchers claim hidden mountainous scenery exists beneath the surface of the *Mona Lisa*; skeptics respond that **X‑ray examinations** show no such hidden scenery.
- **Conclusion (skeptics):** Since X‑ray images reveal no mountains, the researchers’ claims are unfounded.
- **Assumption (skeptics):** X‑ray technology is capable of detecting any underlying paint layers that might include hidden features.
- **Logical gap:** X‑ray images detect only certain pigments; hidden scenery painted with pigments undetectable by X‑ray could remain invisible.
- **Structure:** Counter‑argument (skeptical) presented against a claim of hidden features; weakening this requires challenging the reliability of the method used.

### Question Clues / Keywords
| Keyword / phrase | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **X‑ray examinations** | Imaging technique used to look beneath paint layers | The skeptics’ argument depends on the assumption that X‑rays would reveal hidden images. |
| **capacity to detect lead‑based white pigments** | Limitation of X‑ray analysis to specific pigments | If X‑rays detect only certain pigments, hidden elements painted with other pigments could remain unseen, undermining the skeptics’ objection. |
| **software‑based technologies** | Advanced analytical methods used by researchers | Suggest that other techniques might detect features X‑rays cannot. |

### Logic
The skeptics’ objection is based on the negative X‑ray evidence.  To weaken this objection, we must show that X‑ray images may not detect all underlying features.  Option E explicitly states that X‑ray analysis can only detect lead‑based white pigments.  If the hidden mountains were painted using pigments that X‑rays cannot detect, the absence of an X‑ray image does not refute the researchers’ claim.  Thus, the skeptics’ objection loses force.

### Why the Correct Answer Works
By pointing out a limitation of X‑ray technology, option E undermines the skeptics’ assumption that an X‑ray would reveal any hidden features.  If X‑rays miss certain pigments, then hidden scenery could exist despite the negative X‑ray result.  This restores credibility to the researchers’ claim and weakens the skeptics’ argument.

### Why Other Options Are Wrong
- **A.** Lack of written records about da Vinci painting over the *Mona Lisa* neither supports nor undermines the researchers’ claim.
- **B.** The fact that mountainous backgrounds were common makes hidden mountains plausible but does not directly refute the skeptics’ reliance on X‑rays.
- **C.** Uncertainty about which parts were painted by assistants is irrelevant to whether hidden scenery exists.
- **D.** The failure of infrared photography as well as X‑ray to reveal hidden mountains would strengthen, not weaken, the skeptics’ objection.

### Final Answer
E

### Prompt / Passage
**Acme Company** markets high‑tech recording and videotaping materials for sale to parents for the purpose of secretly recording the activities of their babysitters and nannies while they are away from home.  In defending their products, an Acme representative argued, “More than **75 percent** of the parents buying our surveillance equipment **fire their babysitters or nannies within two weeks of their starting work**.  This proves that we have a nationwide problem with misconduct by all babysitters and nannies.”

### Question Stem
Which of the following, if true, would **most contradict** the conclusion of the Acme representative’s argument?

### Choices
A. Secretly recording or videotaping people without their consent is unconstitutional.  
B. The customers who use Acme Company’s product are representative of the nationwide problem.  
C. Of all the nannies who are fired in this country, more than **75 percent** are illegal aliens.  
D. **Most of the people who use Acme Company’s products suspected their babysitters of misconduct before using the equipment.**  
E. After six months of use, Acme Company’s surveillance equipment tends to break down and become unreliable.

### Correct Answer
**D** — Most of the people who use Acme Company’s products suspected their babysitters of misconduct before using the equipment.

### Argument Map
- **Premise:** More than 75 % of parents who buy Acme’s surveillance equipment fire their babysitters within two weeks.
- **Conclusion:** There is a nationwide problem with misconduct by all babysitters and nannies.
- **Assumption:** The sample of parents who buy Acme’s product is representative of all parents employing babysitters; there is no selection bias.
- **Logical gap:** Parents who suspect misconduct may be more likely to purchase surveillance equipment; the high firing rate may reflect prior suspicions rather than widespread misbehaviour by babysitters.
- **Structure:** Argument by generalisation from a biased sample.

### Question Clues / Keywords
| Keyword / phrase | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **75 percent fire their babysitters** | Observed statistic among product users | The conclusion extrapolates from this statistic to all babysitters. |
| **suspected misconduct before using the equipment** | Pre‑existing beliefs about babysitter behaviour | If parents already suspected misconduct, the high firing rate is due to selection bias rather than widespread misbehaviour. |
| **nationwide problem with misconduct** | Claim that most or all babysitters misbehave | This broad claim rests on the assumption that the sample is representative. |

### Logic
The argument generalises from the experiences of customers who purchase surveillance equipment to all babysitters.  To contradict the conclusion, we must show that the sample is not representative.  Option D reveals that the customers using Acme’s product **already suspected their babysitters of misconduct** before buying the equipment.  Parents who are worried are more likely to monitor their caregivers, so the high firing rate reflects prior suspicion rather than a nationwide problem.  This directly contradicts the conclusion that misconduct is widespread among all babysitters.

### Why the Correct Answer Works
Selection bias undermines the validity of generalisation.  If most customers purchase the equipment because they already suspect problems, the high firing rate says nothing about babysitters in general.  Option D exposes this bias, showing that the sample is not representative and thus the conclusion is unfounded.

### Why Other Options Are Wrong
- **A.** Constitutional concerns about recording are legal issues; they do not address whether misconduct is widespread.
- **B.** Stating that customers are representative would, if anything, support the conclusion rather than contradict it.
- **C.** Information about the immigration status of fired nannies is irrelevant to the representativeness of the sample or the prevalence of misconduct.
- **E.** Equipment reliability after six months does not affect the argument about firing within two weeks; it is a red herring.

### Final Answer
D

### Prompt / Passage
Within the advertising community, debate has long raged over the **effectiveness of humorous commercials**.  Those who advocate using humour to sell products like to point to survey results, which show that **ordinary consumers are almost twice as likely to recall a humorous commercial** as they are to recall a serious commercial.

### Question Stem
Which of the following, if true, **most weakens** the argument made by the advocates of humor in advertising?

### Choices
A. The consumers surveyed about humorous commercials included people considered unlikely to buy the particular products advertised.  
B. According to viewer ratings, the popularity of humorous television programming has been declining in recent years.  
C. Although most consumers surveyed were able to recall viewing humorous commercials, many said they enjoyed the serious commercials more.  
D. For certain types of products, humorous advertising would be inappropriate and potentially offensive.  
E. Although most consumers surveyed were able to recall viewing humorous commercials, **most failed to recall the name of the product advertised**.

### Correct Answer
**E** — Although most consumers surveyed were able to recall viewing humorous commercials, most failed to recall the **name of the product advertised**.

### Argument Map
- **Premise:** Surveys show that consumers are twice as likely to recall humorous commercials as serious commercials.
- **Conclusion (advocates):** Humorous commercials are more effective at selling products.
- **Assumption:** Recall of a commercial translates into effective marketing; remembering the advertisement implies remembering and buying the product.
- **Logical gap:** Consumers may remember the humour but not the brand or product; recall of the ad does not guarantee product awareness or purchase.
- **Structure:** Argument from correlation between recall rate and advertising effectiveness.

### Question Clues / Keywords
| Keyword / phrase | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **recall a humorous commercial** | Remember seeing the advertisement | The advocates equate recall with effectiveness. |
| **failed to recall the name of the product** | Consumers remember the joke but not the brand | Shows that high recall may not convert to product awareness or sales. |
| **humorous commercials** | Advertisements designed to entertain | The debate centres on whether humour aids or distracts from the selling message. |

### Logic
The advocates’ argument hinges on the assumption that memorability implies effectiveness.  Option E directly undermines this by showing that although humorous ads are remembered, consumers do **not remember the product’s name**.  If viewers recall the joke but not the brand, the commercial fails to achieve its purpose.  This weakens the claim that humorous commercials are more effective.

### Why the Correct Answer Works
Advertising is effective only if it leads consumers to recognise and purchase the product.  Option E demonstrates that the key step—brand recall—is missing.  Therefore, the high recall of humorous commercials does not translate into product awareness or sales, undermining the advocates’ position.

### Why Other Options Are Wrong
- **A.** Survey demographics might affect generalisability, but including unlikely buyers does not necessarily weaken the argument about recall translating to effectiveness.
- **B.** The popularity of humorous programming does not directly address whether humorous advertisements are effective.
- **C.** Enjoyment of serious commercials more than humorous ones speaks to preference, not recall or effectiveness.
- **D.** Certain products being unsuitable for humour does not refute the general claim that humour increases effectiveness; it merely limits its applicability.

### Final Answer
E
